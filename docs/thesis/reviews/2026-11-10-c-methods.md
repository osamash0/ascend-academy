## Ch. 4: the text extraction benchmark

### M1 (weakens). The quick "both unusable" verdicts behind "the strongest statement the benchmark makes"

- **Where:** p. 28, `04-content.tex:419-422`; p. 29, `04-content.tex:504-507` (Answer to RQ1).
- **Quoted:** "31 of its 39 duels ended that way, which is the strongest statement the benchmark makes about the text learners read today."
- **The attack:** the votes record the seconds per decision.
  - The 31 "both unusable" verdicts on `current` in the first comparison have a **median of 3.2 s**. 22 of the 31 took under 5 s.
  - That comparison was judged "in one sitting" with 108 duels (`LAB_LOG.md:178`). Over the three thirds of that sitting, median decision time fell from 9.9 to 8.4 to 4.2 s. The share of "both unusable" was 64 %, 50 %, then 72 %.
  - Across all 283 votes, 25 % of decisions took under 5 s and 8 % under 3 s.
  - In three seconds a judge can see a footer or a run-on paragraph but cannot read two transcriptions. These verdicts are glance judgements, and they are concentrated at the end of a long session.
- **Evidence:** `cmp-2026-10-30_8825/votes.jsonl`, field `seconds`, recomputed.
- **Proposed:** report it, and narrow the claim. "In the first comparison 31 of current's 39 duels ended as 'both unusable', most of them decided in under five seconds (median 3.2 s), late in a single sitting of 108 duels; they record that the text looks unusable at a glance, which is what a reader sees first." If there is time, re-judge a random subset of 20 duels with a minimum viewing time, or have a second judge do it, and report the agreement.

### M2 (weakens). The odds rest on three or four direct duels per pair and are read off a model in which 42 % of votes are draws

- **Where:** p. 28, `04-content.tex:459-460`; p. 29, `04-content.tex:498-501`; p. 26, `04-content.tex:375-376`.
- **Quoted:** "pymupdf4llm is preferred about 4.5:1 over it"; "a vision model's transcription is preferred to the current text layer at odds of more than 10:1"; "400 points above the anchor means 10:1 odds of being preferred".
- **The attack:**
  - **Few direct duels.** The direct record against `current` is three or four duels per pair: pymupdf4llm 2 wins and 1 both-unusable; the stronger vision model 3 wins; gpt-4.1-mini 3 wins and 1 both-unusable; MinerU 2 wins and 1 both-unusable. Everything else in these odds comes through the fitted scale, from duels against other methods.
  - **Draws.** 118 of the 283 votes (42 %) are "both unusable" and 24 are "same". The fit counts both kinds as half a win. With that many draws, 400 points is a ratio of expected scores, not a 10:1 chance of being preferred in a duel.
  - **Direction is robust.** A refit without the "both unusable" votes keeps the top four in the same order and widens every gap to `current`.
- **Evidence:** `h2h.py` and `bench2.py` over the four `votes.jsonl` files.
- **Proposed:** "On the fitted scale pymupdf4llm stands 263 points above the current extraction, about 4.5:1 in expected score; the two met directly in three duels (two wins, one both unusable). Draws, 42 % of all votes, count as half a win each, so the odds describe the scale and not a probability of winning a duel." The same applies to the 10:1 sentences.

### M3 (weakens). A page-level bootstrap supports H1 for four methods, not five

- **Where:** p. 27, `04-content.tex:410-412`; p. 26, `:379-384`.
- **Quoted:** "H1 holds for five methods …"; "The bootstrap resamples single votes … Its intervals are therefore likely too narrow".
- **The attack:** the thesis names the dependence between votes and then does not run the obvious check: resample pages, since each page carries 6 to 22 votes. I ran it with 1,000 resamples of the 24 pages and the same fit and prior. The Bonferroni bounds at k = 10 come out as:

  | Method | Vote bootstrap (published) | Page bootstrap |
  |---|---|---|
  | stronger vision model | 1331 | 1325 |
  | gpt-4.1-mini | 1251 | 1243 |
  | pymupdf4llm | 1164 | 1150 |
  | MinerU | 1068 | 1078 |
  | OpenDataLoader | 1030 | **987** |
  | Docling | 983 | 947 |
  | Marker | 958 | 979 |

  Four methods survive. OpenDataLoader, whose pass already rested on round-2 duels (04:415-418), does not. Three other seeds of the vote bootstrap move OpenDataLoader's bound only between 1026 and 1031, so this is the clustering and not noise.
- **Evidence:** `bench.py` (seed 20261110 for pages; the published seed 20261103 for votes reproduces 1331 / 1251 / 1164 / 1068 / 1030 exactly).
- **Proposed:** add after 04:412: "Resampling pages instead of votes, which respects that votes on one page are not independent, keeps H1 for the two vision models, pymupdf4llm and MinerU (bounds 1325, 1243, 1150, 1078) and not for OpenDataLoader (987)." Then use "four" wherever pass (b) N1 now proposes "five pass the corrected test", or say "five, four when pages are resampled".

### M4 (weakens). The benchmark measures a reader's preference for rendered raw output; the chapter says that output is read by models

- **Where:** p. 26, `04-content.tex:337-339` and `:368-370`.
- **Quoted:** "Every later step, the synthesis, the quiz, the retrieval index, reads what it produces"; "asks 'Which would you rather learn from?'"
- **The attack:** construct validity.
  - The chapter's own motivation is that the extracted text feeds synthesis, the quiz and retrieval. The benchmark instead asks a person which raw output they would rather read. Whether a learner ever reads that text unchanged is not stated.
  - Both outputs are shown through the app's Markdown reader (`PROTOCOL.md:108`; deviations 1 and 3 changed how line breaks and HTML are displayed). Methods that emit headings, lists and tables therefore get typography that plain text does not. LAB_LOG names the vision outputs' "consistent Markdown headings" as a reason they may be recognisable (`PROTOCOL.md:232`).
  - Part of the ranking may therefore be layout preference. M1's three-second verdicts point the same way.
  - Nothing measures the downstream quantity, for example retrieval hits or synthesis faithfulness per extractor. The golden set and retrieval harness to do it exist.
- **Proposed:** in §4.5.2 Why or Threats: "The benchmark measures which rendered output a reader prefers. The pipeline's consumers are models: synthesis, quiz and retrieval. Whether the preference carries over to them was not measured; a retrieval run per extractor over the golden set would test it." Repeat one clause of this in the Answer to RQ1.

### M5 (cosmetic). The consistency evidence is thin, and there is a side preference

- **Where:** p. 27, `04-content.tex:395`; p. 26, `:368-370`.
- **Quoted:** "hidden repeats agreed in 12 of 13 cases"; "sides randomised".
- **The attack:**
  - 12 of 13 has an exact 95 % interval of 64 % to 100 %. It shows the judge was not erratic; it does not estimate consistency.
  - In the 141 decisive votes the right side won 86 times (61 %; exact binomial p = 0.011). The protocol's seeded balancing puts each method on the left in about half its duels, so the preference does not favour a method. It does show that something other than content moved the decisions.
  - The sides were set by a seeded balancing walk, not drawn at random (`PROTOCOL.md:217`).
- **Proposed:** "hidden repeats agreed in 12 of 13 cases (95 % interval 64 to 100 %); decisive votes went to the right side in 86 of 141 cases, which the balanced assignment of sides keeps from favouring any method." And "sides balanced by a seeded schedule".

## Ch. 4: the routing study

### M6 (weakens). The 53 labels are all one class

- **Where:** p. 25, `04-content.tex:313-316, 325-331`.
- **Quoted:** "answering for each whether a text model could explain the page from its text alone. 53 were labelled, all as needing the image".
- **The attack:**
  - **No negative class.** The labels have one class only (`routing-labels-2026-11-05.csv`, column `label`: 53 × `image`). The study can say how many labelled pages the rule catches. It cannot say how often the rule sends a page to vision that did not need it. With no negative class, the rule's precision is undefined on this set. "About one vision call per figure page" is a count, not a cost-benefit result.
  - **The label is a belief.** "Whether a text model could explain the page" was answered by judgement; no text model was run on the 55 pages to see whether its explanation failed.
  - **One labeller.** There is no second labeller.
  - **What the chapter already admits.** It admits the fit to the calibration set and the 21 unlabelled pages (04:325-329). It does not admit the missing negative class or the nature of the label.
- **Proposed:** add to "What this does and does not show": "The labels contain no page judged readable from its text, so the study cannot estimate how many of the 87 vision calls are unnecessary; and 'needs the image' is the author's judgement, not a measured failure of a text model on the page." Cheap remedy before hand-in: run the production text model on the 55 pages and grade 10 of its outputs blind against the vision output.

### M8 (weakens). An engine that says nothing scores 16 of the 23

- **Where:** p. v, `abstract.tex:30-32`; p. 40, `05-loop.tex:455-457`; Ch. 7 RQ2 (p. 53).

- **The attack:**
  - 15 of the 23 expectations are "must not appear". Three of the eight "must" expectations are themselves about silence: figures null, reach `few`, and nothing said about arm B.
  - I scored the same expectations for a **mute engine**: one that never reports a finding or a theme but leaves reach and figures as they were. It meets **16 of 23**: 3 of the 8 "must" and 13 of the 15 "must not". The real engine meets 18.

- **Evidence:** `EXPECTATIONS.json` and `*.summary.json`. The scoring reuses `check()` from `a342dea9:backend/eval/persona_study.py:308-331`, with findings and themes emptied.
- **Proposed:** report the expectations by kind. Ch. 5: "Of the five expectations that a finding must appear, four were met; of the eighteen that the view must stay silent or empty, fourteen were met. An engine that never reported anything would meet sixteen of those eighteen, so the silent half of the study tests the floor rules and not detection." ### M9 (weakens). "Written down before the run" has no record

- **Where:** p. 40, `05-loop.tex:420-423`; Table 5.3 caption (`:432`).
- **Quoted:** "Before the first run we wrote down, for every arm, which findings must appear and which must not: 23 expectations in all."
- **The attack:**
  - **No timestamp.** The expectations, the post-hoc expectations, the script, the results and the notes all first appear in one commit (`a342dea9`, 9 November 21:25). The pre-registered and post-hoc expectations live side by side in the same Python file (`persona_study.py:222-238`). Nothing timestamps the 23 before the run.
  - **Compare the other evaluations.** The benchmark protocol (`8f70b7ef`, 20:11 UTC) precedes the first vote (20:37 UTC). The retrieval re-run protocol (`d09c05ea`) precedes its run. The persona study is the one evaluation in the thesis whose "pre-registration" cannot be shown.
  - **Selected predictions.** The code comments show that no expectation was registered for the confusion block or the speed bump in arm A, because the outcome was "uncertain" (`:230-231`). So the registered set leaves out exactly the predictions that could have failed.
- **Proposed:** "The expectations were written in the script before it was first run, in the same working session; the commit that holds them also holds the results, so the order is our statement and not a record. Where we were unsure of an outcome (the block and the speed bump in arm A) we registered none." Keep "pre-registered" for the benchmark and the re-run only.

### M10 (weakens). One seed per arm, with results on the threshold edge

- **Where:** p. 41, `05-loop.tex:514-517`.
- **Quoted:** "there is one Lesson, one Space, and one seed per arm."
- **The attack:**
  - Several outcomes sit next to a threshold. Arm B fires at 23 % confused against a 20 % rule. Arm H's absent speed bump and absent leak depend on 6 of 27 and 5 of 27 people. Arm C's missing themes depend on the hotspot rule.
  - With one seed, the reader cannot tell a stable outcome from a lucky draw. The thesis says the runs "take seconds" (05:409), so 20 seeds per arm would cost a minute.
- **Proposed:** run each arm over 20 seeds and report, per expectation, how often it holds, for example "B's hotspot appears in k of 20 seeds". If there is no time: "Arm B's hotspot rests on 23 % against a rule of 20 %; with one seed per arm we do not know how often it would appear."

### M11 (weakens). The misses are scored against the design that produced them

- **Where:** p. 40, `05-loop.tex:469-472`; Ch. 7 RQ2 (p. 53).
- **Quoted:** "In none of the five misses does the code depart from its design. Each is a rule of the design that our expectations had read wrongly."
- **The attack:**
  - Once "the design" is defined as whatever the code does, no outcome of the study can count as a design failure. The study then becomes a test of the author's understanding of their own code.
  - That is a legitimate result, but not the one RQ2 asks about.
  - Arm B's registered expectation was the privacy promise itself: "three struggle: nothing may be said about them". Its failure is a failure of the design against its stated promise, not a misreading. Pass (b) N3 shows the rule is weaker still: one confused reader in five can carry a hotspot.
- **Proposed:** "In none of the five misses does the code depart from its rules; four misses show rules we had misremembered, and one, arm B, shows that the rules do not keep the promise we wrote down for them."

## Ch. 6: the verifier probe

### M12 (weakens). The verifier that failed faced a registered gate; the one adopted faced none

- **Where:** p. 48, `06-trust.tex:291-292, 308-314`; p. 49, `:357-362`.
- **Quoted:** "We measured both for every verifier of answer keys on the same fixed probe."; "The answer check dropped none of the 16 good keys and kept none of the 16 wrong ones."
- **The attack:**
  - **Rigour for the semantic gate.** It had a written procedure: a threshold fitted on a separate calibration set, the probe scored once, a gate, and consequences fixed in advance.
  - **No such procedure for the answer check.**
    - It was written and measured in one squashed commit (`461afc40`, 30 October), against a probe that had existed since 11 October.
    - The commit records no gate.
    - Its checker model was chosen after the bulk model "passed" LS-115 twice (commit body), and LS-115 is the very question the check was built for. The 5-of-5 on LS-115 is therefore in-sample.
    - Nothing shows the check's prompt was not iterated against the 16 pairs.
  - **Result.** The positive result carries less method than the negative one.
- **Evidence:** `git show 461afc40` (message and files); `06-trust.tex:308-314`.
- **Proposed:** "Unlike the semantic gate, the answer check was not held to a gate registered in advance. It was built for LS-115 and measured in the same change on the existing probe, so its result is in-sample. Before it is called verified, it should be scored once on new pairs from other decks against a gate fixed first."

### M13 (weakens). The wrong keys are easy, and the failure that motivated the check is not in the probe

- **Where:** p. 48, `06-trust.tex:301-306`.
- **Quoted:** "Each pair holds one correct key that paraphrases the page and one wrong key that stays on the topic of the page."
- **The attack:**
  - **Easy wrong keys.** The wrong keys are lexically distant and plainly false. Examples: "Edgar Codd, in the relational prototype Ingres" and "Transactions are always executed on a separate replica server". The probe's own record says "A false-keep rate of zero shows only that lexically distant hallucinations are rejected, not that the verifier cannot be fooled" (`dfc20df6:docs/DECK_LAB_2026_10.md:168`).
  - **The real failure is different.** The production failure that started the answer check (LS-115) was not a wrong key at all: every option was on the page, and the question was ambiguous. The probe has no such case. LS-115 itself is n = 1.
  - **Correct keys built to fail lexical.** They were written "under a strict no-three-word-copy rule" (same file). The lexical score's 14-of-16 is therefore partly a property of how the probe was built.
- **Proposed:** in §6.5.4: "The wrong keys are on the topic but plainly false, so a zero keep rate shows that such keys are rejected, not that subtler errors are; the correct keys were written to avoid three-word overlap, which makes the lexical result partly a product of the probe. The failure that motivated the answer check, an ambiguous question whose options all appear on the page, is represented by one case."

### M14 (cosmetic). The gate limits cannot tell a good verifier from a mediocre one

- **Where:** p. 48, `06-trust.tex:310-311`; Table 6.1 (p. 49).
- **The attack:**
  - 0 of 16 is compatible with a true error rate up to 21 %. 14 of 16 has an interval of 62–98 %, and 8 of 16 of 25–75 %.
  - The gate "at most one wrong key kept of 16" passes a verifier whose true false-keep rate is 10 % with probability 0.52, and one at 15 % with probability 0.28.
  - Chapter 6 says the counts are not error rates (06:393-395) but gives no interval.
- **Proposed:** add the intervals to Table 6.1 or its caption: "0 of 16 is compatible with an error rate of up to 21 % (exact 95 % interval)."

## Ch. 6: the refusal-gate re-run

### M15 (weakens). The answerable half replicates a known result, and the confirmatory bars were low

- **Where:** p. 49, `06-trust.tex:375-388`.
- **Quoted:** "with expectations committed before the run …"; "the gate let all 27 through and refused all 30."
- **The attack:**
  - **A replication.** The 27 answerable questions are the golden-set questions of this Space. In September they had already passed the gate (`retrieval-rerun/PROTOCOL.md:11`: "none of the 27 in-course questions was refused"; E1: "September: 0"). 27 of 27 is a replication on a set known to pass, not a new test. Those questions were also drafted with AI assistance from the slide text (pass (b) W1).
  - **Low bars.** The confirmatory bars for the unanswerable tiers were "at least 9 of 10" and "at least 8 of 10" (E2, E3). Only tier C was open, and its prediction failed in the favourable direction.
  - **Narrow margin.** The highest unanswerable question sits at 0.634 and the lowest answerable one at 0.659, with the threshold at 0.65. A drift of 0.009 in the threshold, or in the embedding model, would refuse an answerable question.
  - **Wide intervals.** 0 of 30 false accepts is compatible with up to 12 %, and 0 of 27 false refusals with up to 13 %.
  - **Credit due.** The threshold was not fitted to these questions: it is production's fixed value. The protocol precedes the run.
- **Proposed:** "The 27 answerable questions are those that passed the gate in September, so this half of the result replicates a known outcome; the new evidence is the 30 unanswerable questions, refused at a margin of 0.025 below the lowest answerable one. Zero errors in 30 is compatible with a false-accept rate of up to 12 %."

## Across the three evaluations

### M16 (weakens). One person in every role, with no second rater anywhere

- **Where:** p. 26, `04-content.tex:389-390`; p. 41, `05-loop.tex:519-521`; p. 50, `06-trust.tex:393`; Ch. 7 Limitation 2.
- **The attack:** in each evaluation the same person did every one of these jobs:
  - built the system;
  - wrote the test material: the probe, the persona expectations, the routing labels, and the golden questions (with AI assistance);
  - judged the outcomes: every benchmark vote;
  - analysed the results.

  The thesis states this, but it never measures what it costs. A second rater on a small subset would turn the limitation into a number. Candidates are 20 benchmark duels, the 55 routing labels, or the 32 probe keys. The co-developer, or any classmate blind to method names, could do it in an hour.
- **Proposed:** run one inter-rater check, for example 20 duels re-judged blind by a second person with Cohen's κ, and report it in §4.5.2. If not: "No second rater was used on any subset, so how far the results depend on the one judge is unknown."

### M17 (cosmetic). "Pre-registered" means three different things

- **Where:** abstract (p. v); `04-content.tex:344`; `05-loop.tex:420, 432`; `06-trust.tex:308, 375`; Ch. 7 (p. 54).
- **The attack:** the word covers three different practices:
  - **Benchmark:** a frozen protocol, committed before the first vote, with dated deviations.
  - **Semantic gate and re-run:** a written procedure committed before the run.
  - **Persona study:** expectations in the same commit as the results (M9).

  An examiner who checks one and finds it solid will assume the others are the same.
- **Proposed:** keep "pre-registered" for the benchmark, the semantic gate and the re-run, each with the commit that fixes it. For the persona study say "expectations written before the first run".

## Dropped after testing (13)

1. **The protocol was frozen after voting began.** No: `8f70b7ef` at 20:11 UTC precedes the first vote at 20:37 UTC on 30 October.
2. **The retrieval re-run protocol came after the run.** No: `d09c05ea` precedes the run.
3. **The fitted threshold of the re-run was tuned to the questions.** No: 0.65 is production's fixed value.
4. **The Bonferroni bound from 1,000 resamples at the 0.25th percentile is unstable.** Three other seeds move OpenDataLoader between 1026 and 1031 and leave every verdict unchanged.
5. **Treating "both unusable" as a draw changes the conclusions.** A refit without those votes keeps the top four in order and puts `current` below every method but MarkItDown. The direction holds; only the odds language is attacked (M2).

7. **Round 2 gave the leaders more duels, inflating their precision.** It did, by the registered rule. The thesis states that OpenDataLoader's pass rests on round 2.
8. **The heavy engines saw a 60-page cut.** Stated in the threats (04:453-454).
9. **pymupdf4llm's OCR pass confounds its rating.** Stated (04:432-434).
10. **The semantic gate's procedure was not really fixed in advance.** The archive tag holds the written procedure and gate. The thesis says the threshold itself was fitted.
11. **The answer check was measured only once.** Stated (06:396-397).
12. **The personas are too clean.** Stated (05:515-516).
13. **The scorecard does not show better quizzes.** Stated (04:476-477); M7 attacks the circularity only.

## The five attacks that matter most

1. **M8 + M9, the persona study.** An engine that never reports anything meets 16 of the 23 expectations against the real engine's 18. Fifteen expectations are absences. The detection half is four of five. The claim that the expectations came first has no record, because they share a commit with the results. Report the study by kind of expectation and drop "pre-registered" for it.
2. **M12 + M13, the answer check.** It is the one verifier the thesis adopts, and the only one measured in-sample, with no registered gate. Its probe's wrong keys are easy, and the failure that motivated it (an ambiguous question) appears once. Say so. Best of all, score it once on fresh pairs against a gate fixed first.
3. **M1, glance verdicts.** "The strongest statement the benchmark makes" rests on "both unusable" verdicts with a median of 3.2 seconds, late in a 108-duel sitting. Report the decision times and narrow the claim.
4. **M3 + M2, the benchmark statistics.** Resampling pages keeps H1 for four methods, not five. The 4.5:1 and 10:1 odds come from a scale in which 42 % of votes are draws, with three or four direct duels per pair. One added sentence and one rewording fix both.
5. **M4, construct validity.** The benchmark measures which rendered raw output a person prefers. The chapter says the text is consumed by synthesis, the quiz and retrieval. Name the gap; a retrieval run per extractor over the existing golden set would close it.

Close behind: M6 (the routing labels have no negative class) and M16 (no second rater anywhere, while 20 re-judged duels would put a number on the largest acknowledged threat).
