\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This chapter collects the answers to the three research questions, discusses
what the work showed beyond them, states its limitations, and looks ahead.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:summary}

\paragraph{RQ1: from PDF to structured, grounded content.}
The transformation is designed for reliability by a first pass that uses no
model. One rule, measured on the
page without its footer, sends 87 of the 1{,}593 pages of the corpus to a
vision model. In the blind benchmark, under a protocol
fixed before the first vote and extended to the vision models after the
first comparison, transcription by a
vision model was rated highest (1452 and 1376 points) and pymupdf4llm third (1263).
Nine of the ten alternatives score above the production extraction; five stay
above it at the corrected lower bound, and four when pages rather than votes
are resampled. 
The limits are one judge without a second rater, born-digital pages from one
university, later comparisons that were less blind, and a preference between
rendered outputs that was not tested on the models that consume the text.
Reliability end to end and the grounding of the generated page text were not
measured.

\paragraph{RQ2: from reader events back to the owner.}
Interaction is modelled as one governed log of 17~event types, which the
Space engine turns into nine kinds of finding per page. The owner's view
names no one and says nothing about a page that fewer than five people have
read. In the study with scripted readers, four of the five
expectations that a finding appears were met, and fourteen of the eighteen
that the view stays silent, where an engine that reports nothing would meet
sixteen; the two misses of arm~B show that the rules do not keep the promise
made to members (\cref{sec:c5-evaluation}). By the criterion of
\cref{sec:analytics-privacy}, that no statement is about fewer than five
people, the view is not yet privacy-preserving: a hotspot can rest on one
confused rater among five, and a policy of the older dashboard still lets an
owner with the professor role read event rows with user ids
(\cref{sec:c5-privacy}).

\paragraph{RQ3: verifying generated content.}
Generated content is verified by computation where an answer can be
computed, by the answer check for the quiz questions written during parsing,
and by structure elsewhere. On 16~fixed pairs the answer check gave no wrong
verdict among the calls that returned one, and one call did not; it was
measured in-sample and without an acceptance criterion fixed in advance. The
lexical score would have dropped 14 of the 16 correct keys, and the
similarity score failed its pre-registered test with 8 of 16 wrong keys kept
and was not adopted. When the check is unavailable, the question is kept. The prose of
explanations and answers is not verified: the ask bar relies on retrieval
and on citations it does not require, and Explain, Ask, and Luna on rules in
the prompt and on the original page next to the text.

\paragraph{The three aims.}
Of the aims in \cref{sec:aims}, the first two are met within the limits of
RQ1 and RQ2, and the third for the readers of a Space, who share its owner's
parse, but not for two uploaders of the same file.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We first set each answer against the work of \cref{ch:background}.

\paragraph{The answers and the literature.}
On RQ1, the benchmark agrees with Dinh et al.\ that the page image carries
more than the text layer~\cite{dinh2025slides}. The agreement is on a
different task, a reader's preference between renderings and not the
accuracy of answers. On RQ2, the floor borrows
the minimum group size of $k$-anonymity~\cite{sweeney2002kanonymity} and
applies it to pages. It guarantees five readers behind a page, not five people behind a finding. On RQ3, the answer check runs on a different model because
model judges favour their own
output~\cite{panickssery2024selfpreference}. The probe compares verifiers and does not test that bias itself.

Six observations follow; they are lessons of one project, not general results.

\paragraph{One live path.}
By the end of May 2026 the upload route switched between four parser paths,
each started to fix the previous one and none removed. Two of the three
tables that no live code writes (\cref{sec:schema-notes}) and several faults
in \cref{ch:content} trace back to that state; in our experience the pipeline
became dependable only when one path was left, which was not measured.

\paragraph{The simple method was never chosen.}
The text extractor in production was what remained when other tools were
removed for memory, cost, or complexity. The benchmark of \cref{ch:content}
was the first comparison it faced, and nine of the ten alternatives were
rated above it. Measuring a method before relying on it applies to the
verifiers as well: the similarity score of \cref{ch:trust} looked right and
failed.

\paragraph{Cost shaped the architecture.}
Free and low-cost quotas explain the ordered provider chains, the spending
caps, parsing once, and the deterministic first pass. Two faults found while
planning the provider chaos tests were faults of cost, now guarded by five
invariants in 11~property tests. For a system that a student can afford to
run, cost is a design requirement from the start.

\paragraph{Agent-written code needs written-down expectations.}
With most commits written by coding agents, the practices that kept the code
in line with what was intended fixed an expectation before the work: a
failing test first, a threshold registered before a run, documented claims
checked against the code, and a pinned revision. Several faults reported here
were found later, where these practices had been skipped.

\paragraph{The system was built ahead of its users.}
Review, exams, badges, and analytics were built for learners who had not
arrived: of twelve flags, four are switched on (\cref{sec:built-vs-deployed}).
Feature toggles are known to accumulate as
debt~\cite{rahman2016toggles,hodgson2017toggles}, and here the debt showed as
menu entries that led to routes answering ``not found'' until, in October,
the build derived each interface flag from its backend flag. The move to a
reading-first interface in October was a late correction of the same habit.

\paragraph{Documentation and reality.}
\label{sec:reality-vs-documented}

The sixth observation is that the documents of the project and the running
system drifted apart, and that the drift was found only by reading code and
probing production. It matters because the three answers rest on statements about the system. The cases verified here are of four
kinds: a description of a gateway that had been removed
(\cref{sec:architecture}); a comment saying that the proxy strips a path prefix it keeps
(\code{nginx.conf:24}); configuration that named a server the system did not
run on (\cref{sec:deployment}); and a production database that differed from
the repository, with a column that no migration defines, a migration history
of only two entries, and a storage bucket that could be listed publicly
(\cref{sec:data-model,sec:hardening}). Such drift is well documented for
larger
teams~\cite{lethbridge2003documentation,aghajani2019docissues,desilva2012erosion};
in a project of one or two people, no second reader compares code, comment,
and document. What helped was to treat the running system as the source of truth and to
delete instead of archive.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations of each evaluation are stated in its chapter. Seven hold for
the thesis as a whole.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{No learners.} No study with participants was run. Nothing in
    this thesis shows that anyone learns more, or more willingly, with the
    system than with the PDF.
  \item \emph{The author is the judge.} The benchmark votes, the routing
    labels, and the 16~pairs of the probe were made by the person who built
    the system, without a second rater. The votes were blind to method names
    within each comparison, but later comparisons were less blind, and the
    selection of pages and pairs was not independent.
  \item \emph{One university, small samples.} All material comes from a few
    courses at one university, born-digital, in two languages. Twenty-four
    pages and sixteen pairs show where a method fails on these pages; they
    do not estimate rates in other material.
  \item \emph{No competing system was run.} The comparison in
    \cref{tab:related} rests on publications.
  \item \emph{The author audited the author's system.} The findings on drift and on security come from reading our own code; a
second reader would find more.
  \item \emph{One machine.} Nothing is known about the behaviour of the
    system under load.
  \item \emph{Two theses, one code base.} The split in \cref{sec:scope} is
    drawn by commits. Design discussions between the two developers left
    no record, so the split of ideas is less sharp than the split of code.
\end{enumerate}

Two questions of the January proposal remain open because they need
participants: how students perceive motivation in a gamified platform, and
whether interactive content improves learning compared to static slides
(\cref{sec:research-questions}). We do not let a quality metric stand in for
them.

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:future-work}

The first step that should follow is the missing study, modelled on the
trial of Learn Your Way~\cite{learnlm2025textbook}: two groups, the same
material, one with the plain PDF and one with the reading view, and a test
directly after and one some days later. Both groups would get the same
practice questions, so that the trial isolates the reading surface, and a
standard usability questionnaire~\cite{brooke1996sus,laugwitz2008ueq}; the
event log of \cref{ch:loop} would supply time on page and revisits.

Four engineering steps follow from the findings. First, pymupdf4llm should
be judged against the current extraction again with its OCR pass off; if it
still leads, it should replace the current call, and the running footer
should be stripped from the stored text. Second, the floor of five should
apply to the people a finding is about, the policy that lets an owner with
the professor role read event rows should be dropped, and the retention job,
which has never run, should be switched on. Third, the parse of a file should
be shared across uploaders, which the content hash already permits and the
ownership model does not yet. Fourth, the loop needs its return channel: a
member's question that reaches the owner, and an owner's answer that reaches
every reader of the page.

The nearest step is a fourth view of a page that rebuilds its layout as text:
its backend (PR~\#182) is merged and switched off, and its part in the
reading view (PR~\#183) has not reached the main branch.

Two wider directions are ideas of the author and not results: teaching
material versioned and shared like source code, and models that a university
runs itself, which would keep learner questions inside the institution.

\section{Closing}
\label{sec:reflection}

The thesis set out to let a lecture PDF answer questions and report how it is
read. In the system as merged, a page can be read at once, explained, asked
about, and practised, and its owner sees where readers struggle without being
shown who they are. The measurements say that the text under these features
should come from a better extractor, that the loop separates scripted
behaviours, and that one of three verifiers failed its test. The habit the
project produced is to write down what is expected, to measure, and to report
the result when it disappoints.
