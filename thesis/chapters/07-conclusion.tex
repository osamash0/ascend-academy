\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This chapter collects the answers to the three research questions, discusses
what the work showed beyond them, states its limitations, and looks ahead.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:summary}

\paragraph{RQ1: from PDF to structured, grounded content.}
The pipeline is designed for reliability by a first pass that uses no model;
its reliability end to end was not measured. The pipeline hashes every page, plans the deck, and decides the route of
each page by one rule: a page with fewer than 25~characters of text outside
its footer, or with a figure of at least 5\,\% of its area and fewer than
150~characters, goes to a vision model. On our corpus this sends 87 of
1{,}593~pages to vision. A file is parsed at most once per uploader and
pipeline version unless a re-parse is forced; a second uploader of the same
bytes during a running parse waits until a sweep fails the run after six
hours (\cref{sec:idempotency}). Whether the generated text of a page is
grounded in that page was not measured: \cref{ch:trust} lists which
generated items are checked, and the page text is not among them. Which
extraction gives the most useful text was measured in a blind benchmark
under a protocol fixed before the first vote: eleven methods, 24~pages from
five PDFs in two languages, and 283~pairwise votes by the author as the only
judge. The vision models entered by a dated deviation after the first
comparison, and the later comparisons were less blind. Transcription by a vision
model is rated highest (ratings of 1452 and 1376), the layout-aware variant of the library that production already uses
is third (1263), and the heavy local converters do not beat it. The
extraction that production runs is the anchor of the scale (1000). Nine of
the ten alternatives are rated above it, five of them by the corrected
test and four when pages are resampled, and it leaks footer text on 23 of 24~pages. The two vision models together cost \$0.21 for the
24~pages. The answer
has a clear limit: one judge, who built the system, with no second rater,
and born-digital pages from one university. The judge chose between rendered
outputs, and whether the models that read the text do better on the
preferred one was not measured.

\paragraph{RQ2: from reader events back to the owner.}
Learner interaction can be modelled as one governed log of 17~event types,
eight of which the reader writes. Ten of eleven detectors run over a window
of 56~days and turn it into nine kinds of finding per page. The owner's view
shows a finding only for a page that at least five people have read and never
names a person. It was merged two days after the pinned revision and is not
yet deployed. Two limits remain. A hotspot needs one confused reader among five who rated
the page, and in the study it rested on three among thirteen; and a database policy of the older dashboard still
lets an owner with the professor role read event rows with user ids
(\cref{sec:c5-privacy}). In a
study with scripted readers in eight arms, 18 of 23~expectations that were
written down before the run were met, nine of them in the control arm: four
of five that a finding must appear, and fourteen of eighteen that the view
must stay silent, of which an engine that reports nothing would meet
sixteen. A
Space with four members shows nothing. A mix of 27~readers surfaces the
confusing page and the gap between confidence and correctness, and nothing
else; this was observed and was not a registered expectation. Four of the
five misses were our own misreadings of the rules, and one shows a rule that
does not keep the promise we had written down for it; each is now a stated
limit
(\cref{sec:c5-evaluation}). The study tests the machinery, with the
summarising model replaced by fixed strings. It says nothing about learners
or about what real themes reveal.

\paragraph{RQ3: verifying generated content.}
Generated content is verified in three ways, and they are not equally good:
by computation, by an independent check, and by structure. Where an answer
can be computed, the system computes it and does not ask a model. For the
quiz questions written at parse time, an independent check on a different
model reads the question against the page. On 16~pinned pairs it disputed no
correct key and accepted no wrong key, which had been written by hand, among
the calls that returned a verdict; one call returned none. The lexical rule
it replaced had rejected 14 of the 16~correct keys. A third verifier of
answer keys, a semantic similarity gate, failed its pre-registered test with
8 of 16~false keeps at a threshold that had been fitted to the calibration
data, and it was withdrawn and not tuned. When the check disputes a key, the
question is rewritten once against the same page; a second dispute drops it.
When the check itself is unavailable, the question is kept, so an outage
lets unchecked questions through. Multiple-choice and ordering questions in
the reader and the rehearsal are checked for shape only and graded against
the model's key. What is not verified at all is the prose of explanations
and answers. The ask bar relies on retrieval and on citations that it does
not require; Explain, Ask, and Luna rely on rules in the prompt and on the
original page next to the text.

\paragraph{The three aims.}
Of the aims in \cref{sec:aims}, the first is met by design: the original
page stays the default view. The second is met in the owner's view, within
the limits named under RQ2. The third is met for the readers of a Space, who
share its owner's parse, and not for two uploaders of the same file.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Six observations cut across the three answers. They come from ten months of
building the system and are lessons of one project, not general results.

\paragraph{One live path.}
By the end of May 2026 the upload route switched between four parser paths
(v2, v3, v4, and external extractors that fed v4). Each had
been started to fix the previous one, and none had been removed. Two of the
three tables that no live code writes (\cref{sec:schema-notes}) and several
faults in \cref{ch:content} trace back to that state. In our experience the pipeline became dependable
only when a single path was left and the others were deleted; this was not
measured.

\paragraph{The simple method was never measured.}
The text extractor in production was never chosen. It was what remained
when other tools were removed for memory, cost, or complexity. The benchmark
of \cref{ch:content} is the first comparison it ever faced, and it lost.
This is the strongest argument in the thesis for measuring before
believing, and it applies to our own verifier as well: the semantic gate of
\cref{ch:trust} looked right and failed.

\paragraph{Cost shaped the architecture.}
Free and low-cost model quotas explain the ordered provider chains, the
spending caps, parsing once, and the deterministic first pass. Two faults
found while planning the provider chaos tests were faults of cost and not of
correctness; five invariants in 11~property tests now pin them. For a
system that a student can afford to run, cost is a design requirement of
the same rank as latency.

\paragraph{Agent-written code needs written-down expectations.}
With most commits written by coding agents, the practices that kept the
system honest were those that fix an expectation before the work: a failing
test first, a threshold registered before a run, a ledger of claims checked
against the code, and a pinned revision. Where we skipped them, the thesis
reports the result as a fault that was found later.

\paragraph{The system was built ahead of its users.}
Review, exams, badges, and analytics were built for learners who had not
arrived: of twelve flags, four are switched on
(\cref{sec:built-vs-deployed}). Feature toggles are known to accumulate as
debt~\cite{rahman2016toggles,hodgson2017toggles}. Here the debt showed:
earlier in the year the flag of the interface and the flag of the backend
could disagree, so that a menu entry led to a route that answered ``not
found''. Two such mismatches existed, and one exam screen had no interface
flag at all. Since the end of October the build derives each interface flag
from its backend flag. The move to a reading-first interface in October was
a correction of the same kind, and it came late.

\subsection{Documentation and Reality}
\label{sec:reality-vs-documented}

The sixth observation is that the documents of the project and the running
system drifted apart, in both directions, and that the drift was found only
by reading code and probing production. The cases that this thesis verified
are of four kinds. Documentation described a design that no longer ran: the
backend's own description still presented a gateway in front of the model
providers after the gateway had been removed (\cref{sec:architecture}).
Comments described behaviour the code did not have: a comment in the proxy
configuration says that a path prefix is stripped, and the rule below it
passes the path on unchanged (\code{nginx.conf:24}); a comment called a
start-up mode supported in which every upload fails, until it was corrected
(\cref{sec:deployment}). Configuration named a place that was not used:
the header of the production file named a university server while the
system ran elsewhere (\cref{sec:deployment}). And the database in
production was not the one in the repository: a column existed that no
migration defines, the migration history recorded two of the migrations,
and a storage bucket that the repository had made private could be listed
publicly (\cref{sec:data-model,sec:hardening}).

Such drift is well documented for larger
teams~\cite{lethbridge2003documentation,aghajani2019docissues,desilva2012erosion}.
In a project of one or two people the same person writes the code, the
comment, and the document at different times, and no second reader compares
them. Two measures helped: to treat the running system as the source of
truth and write the probe into the runbook, and to delete instead of
archive, because every case above involved something that was kept after it
had stopped being true.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations of each evaluation are stated in its chapter. Seven hold for
the thesis as a whole.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{No learners.} No study with participants was run. Nothing in
    this thesis shows that anyone learns more, or more willingly, with the
    system than with the PDF.
  \item \emph{The author is the judge.} The benchmark votes, the labels of
    the routing study, and the 16~pairs of the verification probe were made
    by the person who built the system. Within each comparison the votes
    were blind to the names of the methods. Later comparisons were less
    blind: by then the judge had seen earlier outputs with their names, and
    the output of a vision model can be recognised by its style. The
    selection of pages and pairs was not independent.
  \item \emph{One university, small samples.} All material comes from a few
    courses at one university, born-digital, in two languages. Twenty-four
    pages and sixteen pairs can show that a method fails badly on these
    pages. They do not estimate rates in other material, and the intervals
    of the benchmark are likely too narrow.
  \item \emph{No competing system was run.} The comparison in
    \cref{tab:related} rests on publications.
  \item \emph{The author audited the author's system.} The findings on
    drift and on security were made by reading our own code. A second
    reader would find other things, and probably more.
  \item \emph{One machine.} Nothing is known about the behaviour of the
    system under load.
  \item \emph{Two theses, one code base.} The split in \cref{sec:scope} is
    drawn by commits. Design discussions between the two developers left
    no record, so the split of ideas is less sharp than the split of code.
\end{enumerate}

Two of the four questions of the January proposal remain unanswered: how
students perceive motivation in a gamified platform, and whether
interactive content improves learning efficiency compared to static
slides. Both need participants. We state them as open and do not let a
quality metric stand in for them.

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:future-work}

The first step that should follow is the missing study. Its model is the
trial of Learn Your Way~\cite{learnlm2025textbook}: two groups, the same
material, one with the plain PDF and one with the reader, a test directly
after and one some days later. Our design would give both groups the same
practice questions, so that the trial isolates the reading surface, and it
would add a standard usability
questionnaire~\cite{brooke1996sus,laugwitz2008ueq} for the perception
question. The event log of \cref{ch:loop} would supply time on page and
revisits without further instrumentation.

Four engineering steps follow from the findings. Before production
switches its extraction, the layout-aware variant should be judged against
the current one again with its OCR pass off, since production would not run
that pass. If it still leads, it should replace the current call, and the
running footer should be stripped from the stored text, as the deck plan
already strips it from the model's input. Vision remains the option where
its cost per page and per uploader is accepted. The floor of five should
apply to the people a finding is about, the policy that still lets an owner
with the professor role read event rows should be dropped, and retention
should run. The parse of a
file should be shared across uploaders, which the content hash already
permits and the ownership model does not yet. And the loop needs its return
channel: a member's question that reaches the owner, and an owner's answer
that reaches every reader of the page. One step further is an assistant
that proposes a change to a page for the owner to approve, which would
close the loop on the material itself. No such proposal should be applied
without the owner, for the reasons of \cref{ch:trust}.

Two changes were merged into the main branch after the pinned revision. The
owner's view in the current interface (PR~\#181), on which the answer to RQ2
rests, is merged and not yet deployed. The backend of a third view of a
page, which rebuilds its layout as text (PR~\#182), is merged and switched
off. Its reader part (PR~\#183) was merged into the branch of PR~\#182 and
has not reached the main branch; it is the nearest part of this outlook.

Two wider directions are ideas of the author and not results: teaching
material that is versioned and shared as source code is, and models that a
university runs itself, which would keep learner questions inside the
institution.

\section{Closing}
\label{sec:reflection}

The thesis set out to make a lecture PDF answer back. In the system as
merged, a page can be read at once, explained, asked about, and practised,
and its author can see where readers struggle without being shown who they
are.
The measurements say that the text under these features should come from a
better extractor than the one in production, that the loop separates
scripted behaviours with five misses that are now stated limits, and that
one of three verifiers had to be withdrawn. The
most useful habit the project produced is visible in each of those
sentences: to write down what is expected, to measure, and to report the
result when it is not the hoped-for one.
