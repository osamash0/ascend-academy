\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This chapter collects the answers to the research questions, discusses what the work showed beyond them, states its limitations, and looks ahead.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:summary}

\paragraph{RQ1: from PDF to readable pages.}
We design the transformation for reliability with a first pass that uses no model. One rule, measured on the
page without its footer, sends 87 of the 1{,}593 pages of the corpus to a
vision model. In the blind benchmark, under a protocol
fixed before the first vote and extended to the vision models after the
first comparison, the author rated transcription by a vision model highest (1452 and 1376 points) and pymupdf4llm third (1263).
Nine of the ten alternatives score above the production extraction on points,
five at the corrected lower bound, and four when we resample pages rather than votes.
The limits are one judge without a second rater, born-digital pages from one
university, later comparisons that were less blind, and a preference between
rendered outputs that we did not test on the models that consume the text. We measured neither end-to-end reliability nor the grounding of the page text.

\paragraph{RQ2: from learner events back to the owner.}
We model interaction as one governed log of 17~event types, which the
Space engine turns into findings per page, of which nine kinds can reach
the owner. The owner's view
names no one and says nothing about a page that fewer than five people have
read. In the study with scripted readers, the view reported every planted
behaviour at each of 100 seeds and said nothing about a Space of four. Its
count of met expectations, 18 of 23 in the published run and a median of 17
over the seeds, says little, because an engine that reports nothing meets 16.
The hotspot of arm~B, three confused readers among thirteen at every seed,
shows that the rules do not keep the promise made to members
(\cref{sec:c5-evaluation}). It shows no individual
learner. Against the stricter standard of \cref{sec:analytics-privacy}, that
no statement is about fewer than five people, it falls short: a hotspot can
rest on one confused rater among five, the floor counts accounts that an owner can add, and a policy of the older dashboard still lets an
owner with the professor role read event rows with user ids
(\cref{sec:c5-privacy}).

\paragraph{RQ3: checking generated content.}
The system verifies generated content by computation where it can compute an answer, by the answer check for the quiz questions that a model writes during parsing, and by structure elsewhere. On 16~fixed pairs the answer check gave no wrong
verdict among the calls that returned one, and one call did not. We measured it in-sample, without an acceptance criterion fixed in advance, and with distractors that are off the subject of the page. The
lexical score would have dropped 14 of the 16 correct keys, and the
similarity score failed its pre-registered test with 8 of 16 wrong keys kept, and we did not adopt it. When the check is unavailable, the system keeps the question. Nothing verifies the prose of explanations and answers: the ask bar relies on retrieval
and on citations it does not require, and Explain, Ask, and Luna on rules in
the prompt and on the original page next to the text.

\paragraph{The three aims.}
Of the aims in \cref{sec:aims}, the system meets the first two within the limits of RQ1 and RQ2, and the third for the learners of a Space, who share its owner's
parse, but not for two uploaders of the same file.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The observations below are lessons of one project, not general results.

\paragraph{The answers and the literature.}
On RQ1, the benchmark agrees with Dinh et al.\ that the page image carries
more than the text layer~\cite{dinh2025slides}. The agreement is on a
different task, a reader's preference between renderings and not the
accuracy of answers. On RQ2, the floor borrows
the minimum group size of $k$-anonymity~\cite{sweeney2002kanonymity} and
applies it to pages. It guarantees five readers behind a page, not five people behind a finding. On RQ3, the answer check runs on a different model, as the work on
self-preference advises~\cite{panickssery2024selfpreference}, although the
reason the project recorded was its own measurement
(\cref{sec:c6-lifecycle}). The probe compares verifiers and does not test that bias itself.

\paragraph{One live path.}
By the end of May 2026 the upload route switched between four parser paths,
each of which a developer started to fix the previous one, and none of which anyone removed. Two of the three
tables that no live code writes (\cref{sec:schema-notes}) and several faults
in \cref{ch:content} trace back to that state. In our experience the pipeline
became dependable only when one path remained, which we did not measure.

\paragraph{Nobody chose the simple method.}
The text extractor in production is what remained when we removed other tools for memory, cost, or complexity. The benchmark of \cref{ch:content}
was the first comparison it faced, and the author rated nine of the ten alternatives above it on points, five of them also at the corrected lower bound. Measuring a method before
relying on it applies to the verifiers as well: the similarity score of
\cref{ch:trust} looked right and failed.

\paragraph{Cost shaped the architecture.}
Free and low-cost quotas explain the ordered provider chains, the spending
caps, parsing once, and the deterministic first pass. Two faults found while
planning the provider chaos tests were faults of cost, which five invariants now guard in 11~property tests. For a system that a
student can afford to run, cost is a design requirement from the start.

\paragraph{Agent-written code needs written-down expectations.}
With about 77\,\% of the commits written by coding agents, the practices that kept the code
in line with the intention fixed an expectation before the work: a
failing test first, a threshold registered before a run, documented claims
checked against the code, and a pinned revision. We found several of the faults reported here later, where we had skipped these practices.

\paragraph{The system ran ahead of its users.}
We built review, exams, badges, and analytics for learners who had not arrived: production switches on four of twelve flags (\cref{sec:built-vs-deployed}). Feature toggles accumulate as debt~\cite{rahman2016toggles,hodgson2017toggles}, and here the debt showed as
menu entries that led to routes answering ``not found'' until, in October,
the build derived each interface flag from its backend flag. The move to a
reading-first interface in October was a late correction of the same habit.

\paragraph{Documentation and reality.}
\label{sec:reality-vs-documented}

The documents of the project and the running system drifted apart, and we
found the drift only by reading code and probing production. This matters
because the three answers rest on statements about the system. The verified
cases include a description of a gateway that we had removed
(\cref{sec:architecture}), a comment saying that the proxy strips a path
prefix it keeps (\code{nginx.conf:24}), and configuration that named a server
the system did not run on (\cref{sec:deployment}). The production
database also differs from the repository: it has a column that no migration
defines, a migration history of only two entries, and an event table without
the declared partitions. Until October it also had a storage bucket from which
anyone could download every lecture PDF
(\cref{sec:data-model,sec:hardening}). The literature documents such drift for larger teams~\cite{lethbridge2003documentation,aghajani2019docissues,desilva2012erosion}.
In a project of one or two people, no second reader compares code, comment,
and document. What helped was to treat the running system as the source of truth and to
delete instead of archive.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Each chapter states the limitations of its evaluation. These hold for
the thesis as a whole.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{No learners.} We ran no study with participants. Nothing in
    this thesis shows that anyone learns more, or more willingly, with the
    system than with the PDF.
  \item \emph{The author is the judge.} The person who built the system made the benchmark votes, the routing labels, and the 16~pairs of the probe, without a second rater. The votes were blind to method names
    within each comparison, but later comparisons were less blind, and the
    selection of pages and pairs was not independent.
  \item \emph{One university, small samples.} All material comes from a few
    courses at one university, born-digital, in two languages. Twenty-four
    pages and sixteen pairs show where a method fails on these pages but
    do not estimate rates in other material.
  \item \emph{No competing system.} The comparison in \cref{tab:related} rests on publications only.
  \item \emph{The author audited the author's system.} The findings on drift and on security come from reading our own code and from
read-only probes of production. A second reader would find more.
  \item \emph{One machine.} We know nothing about the behaviour of the
    system under load.
  \item \emph{Two theses, one code base.} We draw the split in \cref{sec:scope} by commits. Design discussions between the two developers left
    no record, so the split of ideas is less sharp than the split of code.
\end{enumerate}

Two questions of the January proposal remain open because they need
participants: how students perceive motivation in a gamified platform, and
whether interactive content improves learning compared to static slides
(\cref{sec:research-questions}).

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:future-work}

The first step that should follow is the missing study, modelled on the
trial of Learn Your Way~\cite{learnlm2025textbook}: two groups, the same
material, one with the plain PDF and one with the reading view, tested right after and again days later. Both groups would get the same
practice questions, so that the trial isolates the reading surface, and a
usability questionnaire~\cite{brooke1996sus,laugwitz2008ueq}, and the
event log of \cref{ch:loop} would supply time on page and revisits.

The findings suggest engineering steps. First, a new comparison should judge pymu\-pdf4llm against the current extraction with its OCR pass off. If pymupdf4llm still leads, it should replace the current call, and the parser should strip the running footer from the stored text. Second, the floor of five should
apply to the people a finding is about, the prepared fixes for the two paths around the owner's view should be applied (\cref{sec:c5-privacy}), the project should close the database paths that \cref{tab:deployed} lists, and switch on the retention job, which first needs the partitions that production lacks. Third, uploaders of the same file should share its parse, which the content hash already permits and the
ownership model does not yet. Fourth, the loop needs its return channel: a
member's question that reaches the owner, and an owner's answer that reaches
every reader of the page.

The nearest step is a fourth view of a page that rebuilds its layout as text. Its backend (PR~\#182) is on the main branch and off, and its part in the
reading view (PR~\#183) is not.

Three wider directions are our ideas, not results. The first would close the
loop of \cref{ch:loop} inside the software. An assistant, the platform's own
or one the owner already uses, would read the owner's view, propose changes
to the pages a finding points to, and apply them only after the owner
approves, keeping each change as a new version of the material, versioned
and shared like source code. Such an assistant must see no more than the
owner sees: the page facts behind the floor, never the event log. The route
that lets a lecturer regenerate the generated text of one page with a short
instruction (\code{ai\_content.py:328--337}) is a first piece of it. The
second direction is models that a university runs itself, keeping learner
questions inside the institution. The third would give the learner a loop of
their own. In \cref{ch:loop} what learners do on a page returns to its owner,
who may change the page. What one learner does could also return to that
learner. A page they marked \emph{Still foggy}, or asked about with Ask, could
start a study session with Luna on that page and become one of the practice
cards that Luna schedules for later (\cref{sec:built-vs-deployed}). Today
neither happens: Luna plans a session without these signals
(\code{luna/prompts.py:114--119}), and her cards come only from her own
sessions (\code{luna/store.py:230--242}). Only the learner would see this
loop. We have not built or tested it.

\section{Closing}
\label{sec:reflection}

The thesis set out to let a lecture PDF answer questions and report how people read it. In the system as merged, a learner can open a page at once, have it explained, ask about it, and practise it, and its owner sees where readers struggle without the view naming them. The measurements say that the text under these features
should come from a better extractor, that the loop separates scripted
behaviours, and that one of three verifiers failed its test. The habit the
project produced is to write down the expectation, to measure, and to report
the result when it disappoints.
