\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Most university teaching still travels as a PDF. A lecturer writes slides,
exports them, and uploads the file to a learning management system. Students
download it and read it alone. From that moment the material is silent in
both directions. The file cannot answer a question, cannot check whether a reader understood a page, and cannot tell the lecturer that half of the readers
stopped at page twelve.

Large language models (LLMs) have made it cheap to add an assistant to such a file.
This helps the individual reader, but nothing ties the answer to the page it came from, every student repeats the work, and the lecturer learns nothing from it. The research on intelligent textbooks has described the
alternative for decades: reading material that carries its own structure,
practice, and record of use~\cite{brusilovsky2022return}. What is new is that
a language model can derive much of that structure from an ordinary PDF.

This thesis describes Learnstation, a web application that the author built with a fellow student to take that
step for lecture material. A lecturer or a student uploads a PDF and
reads it at once. While they read, the system turns each page into objects
that software can address: a text of the page, a vector for search, and, for
selected pages, questions about it (\cref{fig:transformation}). An assistant
called Luna works on these objects, next to the page they come from. The system returns what readers do on the pages to the owner of the material as patterns about pages, with no names and only for pages that at least five
people have read.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{f1-transformation}%
  \caption{The transformation this thesis addresses. The system keeps one PDF as the reading surface and turns it into independently addressable learning objects. Every capability students use in production builds on one of them.}%

(\cref{sec:scope}) had observed as students, not from a survey (\cref{tab:problems}).

The mechanisms create a sixth problem: text and questions that a language
model writes can be wrong. The system therefore has to say how it checks them and
what it does when a check fails.

\section{Aims and Contributions}
\label{sec:aims}

The thesis has three aims: to transform static lecture material into
structured content while the original page stays the reference, to feed what
learners do back to the owner of the material without naming any learner, and to generate
shared content once and not once per reader.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A deterministic first pass that decides without a model call which
    pages need a vision model (\cref{ch:content}).
  \item A blind benchmark of eleven text extraction methods on 24~lecture
    pages in 283~pairwise votes, under a protocol frozen before the first
    vote and extended by four recorded deviations (\cref{ch:content}).
  \item A feedback loop from reader to owner whose view names no person and
    says nothing about a page that fewer than five people have read
    (\cref{ch:loop}).
  \item Verification of generated questions by computation and by an independent answer check, with one verifier that failed its test and that we did not adopt (\cref{ch:trust}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Delimitation}
\label{sec:scope}

This is an engineering thesis. It describes a system that runs in production
and evaluates its parts with curated data. In November 2026 the production database held 55 accounts on real mail or
university domains among 216, our own included. Two had been
active in the previous seven days, one of them ours
(\code{docs/\allowbreak thesis/\allowbreak evidence/}). We ran no user study, and no claim
in this thesis is about what students learn.

Two students built Learnstation, and each of them submits a thesis on the shared code base. The co-developer created the repository on
23~January 2026, and 101 of its commits on all branches are theirs, the last on
28~August 2026. Their work includes the first content filter in three layers
(commit \code{bfbe6c09}), the integration of the Groq provider
(\code{76aa4b42}), and the first anonymised analytics for lecturers
(\code{3abfaa54}, \code{52ead4b5}). They also wired external extractors into
the fourth parser pipeline (\code{8aa255e2}) and made about a dozen security
fixes in July 2026. This thesis covers the parts that came after or replaced them: the
page-by-page pipeline and its benchmark, the event engine and the owner's
view, the verification of generated questions, and the interface in which
students read. Where a chapter describes something the co-developer wrote, it
says so.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method-note}

We follow a build-and-evaluate approach. Each of the three main chapters
describes what we built and ends with an evaluation. The extraction benchmark, the similarity verifier, and the re-run of the
refusal gate had their expectations committed before the run. For the study with scripted readers we wrote them first but committed them with the results. The routing study, the deck-plan scorecard, and the answer check had
none. \emph{We} is the voice of the text. What the author did alone, such as judging and labelling, we attribute to \emph{the author}. We checked statements about the system against the code at the revision named above, unless a section names a later
one, and against production where the two can differ. Where the repository does
not record a reason for a decision, we say so and do not supply one.

AI coding agents generated most of the code under the author's direction, and the author reviewed their changes and decided what to keep. The project's development timeline counts 1{,}229 of
1{,}599~non-merge commits up to November 2026, about 77\,\%, as the work of an agent. For 111 of them the timeline infers this from the committing account. A language model helped the author draft and revise the text of this thesis and helped review it. The author checked and decided everything.
We consider this part of the method and not a footnote to it, because we adopted practices in the later chapters, such as writing the test first, pre-registering thresholds, and pinning a revision, to keep agent-written code honest.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How can heterogeneous lecture PDFs be transformed into
    structured pages that a learner can read at once, and how do text
    extraction methods compare in the quality of their text and their cost?
  \item[RQ2] How can learner interaction events be modelled, aggregated, and
    fed back to content owners without showing any individual learner, and
    what can such a view tell an owner about their material?
  \item[RQ3] How can the quality of LLM-generated learning content be
    checked before learners see it, and what happens when a check fails?
\end{description}

RQ1 leads from the first aim to the routing study, the benchmark, and the scorecard of \cref{ch:content}.
RQ2 leads from the first and third problems and the second aim to the study
with scripted readers in \cref{ch:loop}, and RQ3 from the sixth problem to
the probe and the refusal gate of \cref{ch:trust}. The third aim has no
question of its own. It is a property of the design, which we describe and do
not measure.
