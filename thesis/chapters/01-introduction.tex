\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Most university teaching still travels as a PDF. A lecturer writes slides,
exports them, and uploads the file to a learning management system. Students
download it and read it alone. From that moment the material is silent in
both directions. The file cannot answer a question, cannot check whether a
page was understood, and cannot tell its author that half of the readers
stopped at page twelve.

Large language models have made it cheap to add an assistant to such a file.
A student can paste a page into a chat window and ask for an explanation.
This helps the individual reader, but it leaves three things as they were.
The answer is not tied to the page it came from, so nobody can check it
against the material. The work is repeated by every student for the same
page. And the author learns nothing from it, because the conversation happens
elsewhere. The research on intelligent textbooks has described the
alternative for decades: reading material that carries its own structure,
practice and record of use~\cite{brusilovsky2022return}. What is new is that
a language model can derive much of that structure from an ordinary PDF.

This thesis describes Learnstation, a web application that we built to make
that step for lecture material. A lecturer or a student uploads a PDF and
reads it at once. While they read, the system turns each page into objects
that software can address: a text of the page, the ideas on it, questions
about it, and a vector for search (\cref{fig:transformation}). An assistant
called Luna works on these objects and always points back to the page. What
readers do on the pages is recorded and returned to the owner of the material
as patterns across at least five people.

\thesisfigure{f1-transformation}
  {The transformation this thesis addresses. One PDF is kept as the reading
   surface and turned into independently addressable learning objects; every
   capability students use in production is built on one of them.}
  {fig:transformation}

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

We started from five problems that we observed in our own courses. They are
observations of the author and the co-developer as students, not the result
of a survey. \Cref{tab:problems} lists them with the mechanism that addresses
each one and the research question that examines it.

The mechanisms create a sixth problem of their own. Text and questions that a
language model writes can be wrong, and a wrong answer key in a quiz is worse
than no quiz. A system that generates learning content therefore has to say
how it checks that content and what it does when the check fails. This is the
subject of the third research question.

\section{Aims and Contributions}
\label{sec:aims}

The thesis has three aims. The first is to transform static lecture material
into structured content while keeping the original page intact as the
reference. The second is to feed what learners do back to the author of the
material without exposing any learner. The third is to generate shared
content once and not once per reader; in the system as built, ``once'' means
once per uploader, and \cref{ch:content} states this limit.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A deterministic first pass over a PDF that decides, without a model
    call, which pages need a vision model, combined with parsing once per
    uploader and one shared retrieval index (\cref{ch:content}).
  \item A pre-registered blind benchmark of eleven text extraction methods on
    24~lecture pages, judged in 283~pairwise votes (\cref{ch:content}).
  \item A closed feedback loop from reader to owner that makes no statement
    about a person and none about fewer than five readers, tested with
    scripted readers (\cref{ch:loop}).
  \item Verification of generated questions by computation where an answer
    can be computed, and by an independent check on a different model where
    it cannot, with one verifier that failed its test and was withdrawn
    (\cref{ch:trust}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Delimitation}
\label{sec:scope}

This is an engineering thesis. It describes a system that runs in production
and evaluates its parts with curated data. It does not measure learning
outcomes. On 30~October 2026 the production database held 103~accounts that
are not test accounts, and two of them had been active in the previous seven
days. No user study was run, and no claim in this thesis is about what
students learn.

Learnstation was built by two students, and each of them submits a thesis on
the shared code base. The repository was created by the co-developer on
23~January 2026; of its commits, 101 on all branches are theirs, the last on
28~August 2026. Their work includes the first content filter in three layers
(commit \code{bfbe6c09}), the integration of the Groq provider
(\code{76aa4b42}), the first anonymised analytics for lecturers
(\code{3abfaa54}, \code{52ead4b5}), an early orchestrator for several
providers (\code{8aa255e2}, \code{86270c2c}), and about 30~security fixes in
July 2026. This thesis covers the parts that came after or replaced them: the
page-by-page pipeline and its benchmark, the event engine and the owner's
view, the verification of generated questions, and the interface in which
students read. Where a chapter describes something the co-developer wrote, it
says so.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method-note}

We follow a build-and-evaluate approach. Each of the three main chapters
states requirements, describes what was built, and ends with an evaluation
whose expectations were written down before the run where that was possible.
Every factual statement about the system was checked against the code at the
revision named above, and against the running production system where the
two can differ. Where the repository does not record a reason for a
decision, we say that it is not recorded and do not supply one.

Most of the code was written with AI coding agents under the author's
direction. The project's development timeline counts 1{,}229 of
1{,}599~non-merge commits up to 4~November 2026, about 77\,\%, as written
with an agent; for 111 of them this is inferred from the committing account
and not from a marker in the commit. The share rose from none in the first
of ten phases to more than 90\,\% in the last three. The text of this thesis
was drafted with the help of a language model and then revised by the author.
We consider this part of the method and not a footnote to it, because several
practices in the later chapters, such as writing the test first,
pre-registering thresholds, and pinning a revision, were adopted to keep
agent-written code honest.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The thesis began as a project proposal in January 2026, presented to the
research group together with the co-developer. The proposal asked four
questions: which profiling metrics characterise learning interaction data,
how students perceive motivation in a gamified platform, how interaction
events can be aggregated and analysed, and whether interactive content
improves learning efficiency compared to static slides. Two of them need
participants. Real use of the platform after its launch stayed close to zero,
so these two cannot be answered, and \cref{ch:conclusion} returns to them as
future work. The question about events survives as RQ2. The questions about
content and about trust are new in this thesis.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How can heterogeneous lecture PDFs be reliably transformed into
    structured, grounded educational content, and which extraction methods
    give learners the most useful text at what cost?
  \item[RQ2] How can learner interaction events be modelled, aggregated and
    fed back to content owners in a privacy-preserving way, and what can an
    owner learn from them without seeing a single student?
  \item[RQ3] How can the quality of LLM-generated learning content be
    verified and measured, and what happens when verification fails?
\end{description}
