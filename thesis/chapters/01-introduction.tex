\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Most university teaching still travels as a PDF. A lecturer writes slides,
exports them, and uploads the file to a learning management system. Students
download it and read it alone. From that moment the material is silent in
both directions. The file cannot answer a question, cannot check whether a
page was understood, and cannot tell its author that half of the readers
stopped at page twelve.

Large language models have made it cheap to add an assistant to such a file.
This helps the individual reader, but the answer is not tied to the page it
came from, the work is repeated by every student, and the author learns
nothing from it. The research on intelligent textbooks has described the
alternative for decades: reading material that carries its own structure,
practice, and record of use~\cite{brusilovsky2022return}. What is new is that
a language model can derive much of that structure from an ordinary PDF.

This thesis describes Learnstation, a web application that we built to make
that step for lecture material. A lecturer or a student uploads a PDF and
reads it at once. While they read, the system turns each page into objects
that software can address: a text of the page, a vector for search, and, for
selected pages, questions about it (\cref{fig:transformation}). An assistant
called Luna works on these objects, next to the page they come from. What
readers do on the pages is recorded. A view for the owner of the material
returns it as patterns about pages, with no names and only for pages that at
least five people have read. This view was merged two days after the
revision that the thesis describes, and \cref{ch:loop} shows that a finding
in it can still rest on fewer than five people, in the extreme on one.

   surface and turned into independently addressable learning objects; every

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

We started from five problems that the author and the co-developer observed
as students; they are not the result of a survey. \Cref{tab:problems} lists
them with the mechanism that addresses each one.

    \toprule
    \textbf{Problem} & \textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Examined in} \\
    \midrule
    The author gets no feedback on the material. &
      An event log, ten detectors that speak about pages, and an owner's view

      owner's side: RQ2, \cref{ch:loop} \\
    The cost of processing a file is paid again and again. &
      A file is parsed at most once per uploader and feeds one shared search

      Practice questions, spaced review, and a ledger of experience points. &

  \end{tabular}

The mechanisms create a sixth problem of their own. Text and questions that
a language model writes can be wrong, so a system that generates learning
content has to say how it checks that content and what it does when the
check fails.

\section{Aims and Contributions}
\label{sec:aims}

The thesis has three aims: to transform static lecture material into
structured content while the original page stays the reference, to feed what
learners do back to the author without naming any learner, and to generate
shared content once and not once per reader.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A deterministic first pass over a PDF that decides without a model
    call which pages need a vision model, with parsing at most once per
    uploader
    (\cref{ch:content}).
  \item A blind benchmark of eleven text extraction methods on 24~lecture
    pages, judged in 283~pairwise votes under a protocol that was fixed
    before the first vote and extended four times (\cref{ch:content}).
  \item A feedback loop from reader to owner whose view makes no statement
    about a person and none about a page that fewer than five people have
    read; where this rule is weaker than it sounds is stated in
    \cref{sec:c5-privacy,sec:c5-findings}.
  \item Verification of generated questions by computation and by an
    independent check, with one verifier that failed its test and was
    withdrawn (\cref{ch:trust}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Delimitation}
\label{sec:scope}

This is an engineering thesis. It describes a system that runs in production
and evaluates its parts with curated data. In November 2026 the
production database held 216~accounts. Of these, 161 use test,
demonstration, or invented addresses. The other 55 are on real mail or
university domains and include the developers' own; two of the 55 had been
active in the previous seven days, one of them a developer
(\code{docs/\allowbreak thesis/\allowbreak evidence/}). No user study was run, and no claim
in this thesis is about what students learn.

Learnstation was built by two students, and each of them submits a thesis on
the shared code base. The repository was created by the co-developer on
23~January 2026; of its commits, 101 on all branches are theirs, the last on
28~August 2026. Their work includes the first content filter in three layers
(commit \code{bfbe6c09}), the integration of the Groq provider
(\code{76aa4b42}), the first anonymised analytics for lecturers
(\code{3abfaa54}, \code{52ead4b5}), the wiring of external extractors into
the fourth parser pipeline (\code{8aa255e2}), and about a dozen security
fixes in July 2026. This thesis covers the parts that came after or replaced them: the
page-by-page pipeline and its benchmark, the event engine and the owner's
view, the verification of generated questions, and the interface in which
students read. Where a chapter describes something the co-developer wrote, it
says so.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method-note}

We follow a build-and-evaluate approach. Each of the three main chapters
describes what was built and ends with an evaluation. The extraction
benchmark, the semantic gate of the verifier probe, and the re-run of the
refusal gate fixed their expectations in a commit before the run. The
expectations of the persona study were written before its first run, but
they share a commit with its results. The routing study, the deck-plan
scorecard, and the answer check had no expectations fixed in advance. Statements about the system were checked
against the code at the revision named above, unless a section names a later
one, and against production where the two can differ. Where the repository does
not record a reason for a decision, we say so and do not supply one.

Most of the code was written with AI coding agents under the author's
direction. The project's development timeline counts 1{,}229 of
1{,}599~non-merge commits up to 4~November 2026, about 77\,\%, as written
with an agent; for 111 of them this is inferred from the committing account. The text of this thesis
was drafted with the help of a language model and then revised by the author.
We consider this part of the method and not a footnote to it, because several
practices in the later chapters, such as writing the test first,
pre-registering thresholds, and pinning a revision, were adopted to keep
agent-written code honest.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The thesis began as a project proposal in January 2026. Two of its four
questions need participants and remain open (\cref{sec:limitations}); its
question about interaction events survives as RQ2.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How can heterogeneous lecture PDFs be reliably transformed into
    structured, grounded educational content, and which extraction methods
    give learners the most useful text at what cost?
  \item[RQ2] How can learner interaction events be modelled, aggregated, and
    fed back to content owners in a privacy-preserving way, and what can an
    owner learn from them without seeing a single student?
  \item[RQ3] How can the quality of LLM-generated learning content be
    verified and measured, and what happens when verification fails?
\end{description}

\Cref{ch:background,ch:system} give the background and the system as a
whole, \cref{ch:content,ch:loop,ch:trust} answer the three questions in
order, and \cref{ch:conclusion} discusses the work and its limitations.
