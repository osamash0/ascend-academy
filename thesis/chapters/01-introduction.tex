\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

In the courses we attended, teaching material comes as a PDF. A lecturer
exports the slides of one lecture as one PDF, which this thesis calls a
\emph{deck}, and uploads it to a learning management system. Students
download the file and read it alone. From then on the file cannot check
whether a learner understood a page, and the lecturer cannot see that half of
the learners stopped at page twelve.

Research on intelligent textbooks describes the opposite: textbooks that
model their own content, embed practice, and record how they are
used~\cite{brusilovsky2022return}. Large language models (LLMs) are neural
networks that are trained on large amounts of text and write text in reply to
an instruction. Recent systems use them to turn textbooks and slide decks into
new learning material~\cite{learnlm2025textbook,zhou2025slide2text}. Below,
\emph{model} means a language model, and a \emph{vision model} is a language
model that also reads images.

We began with summaries and explanations: from February 2026 a model rewrote
every page at upload and summarised it (\code{4c23d254}). Existing tools
already explain a document on demand, and in our experience they do it very
well. We saw little that we could add there. This thesis therefore studies
three other steps: turning a lecture PDF into structured pages, returning what
readers do to the person who uploaded the material, and checking the quiz
questions that a model writes before learners see them.

Learnstation is the web application that the author and a fellow student
built. Anyone can upload a PDF to it and read the file at once. While they
read, the system turns each page into a record with a title, a text, and a
summary, writes a short quiz for the deck, and records what readers do on each
page. The owner of the material, who uploaded it, sees what its readers do as
patterns about pages, without names. \Cref{tab:vocabulary} gives the words
that the rest of the thesis uses for the system.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Questions}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:research-questions}

Each step meets a problem that leads to one research question with its own
chapter.

The first problem is the file itself. A PDF records where characters and
images sit on a page, not which text is a heading or in which order a person
reads it. On a slide, much of the meaning often lies in a figure
(\cref{sec:document-ai}). Lecture decks are also \emph{heterogeneous}: one
deck mixes dense text, formulas, diagrams drawn with lines, and pictures
(\cref{sec:c4-requirements}). A learner should be able to read at once while
the system recovers the structure of the pages. The methods that recover the
text of a page differ in quality and in cost. We had never measured our own
choice among them before the benchmark of this thesis
(\cref{sec:parser-bench}).

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How can heterogeneous lecture PDFs be transformed into
    structured pages that a learner can read at once, and how do text
    extraction methods compare in the quality of their text and their cost?
\end{description}

The second problem is that the owner learns nothing about how the material is
read. A system can record what learners do on each page as \emph{events}, such
as reading a page or answering a question. Such events could show which page
confuses readers, where readers leave, and which pages they skip. They are
personal data, however, and in a Space of twenty the owner may know every
learner (\cref{sec:analytics-privacy}).

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ2] How can learner interaction events be modelled, aggregated, and
    fed back to the owner of the material without showing any individual
    learner, and what can such a view tell an owner about their material?
\end{description}

The third problem is the quiz. A model can write multiple-choice questions for
every deck, but a question can carry a wrong \emph{answer key}, the option
marked as correct. A wrong key marks a correct learner wrong, and the learner
has no reason to doubt it. A check of the keys can itself err in two
directions: it can drop a correct question or keep a wrong one. In
Learnstation no person approves a question before learners see it, so
automatic checks are the only line between the model and the learner
(\cref{sec:review}).

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ3] How reliably can the answer keys of quiz questions that a
    language model generates be checked before learners see them, and what
    happens when a check fails?
\end{description}

The questions grew out of a project proposal of January 2026,
\enquote{Discovering Impact: characterizing learning interaction data in
educational contexts}. Its question on how to aggregate interaction events
became RQ2. Its other questions needed interaction data from real learners,
and almost none arrived (\cref{sec:scope}). Two of them, on motivation in a
gamified platform and on whether interactive content improves learning, need
a study with participants and stay open (\cref{sec:limitations}). We do not
pursue its question on metrics for profiling interaction data, because there
is almost none to profile. The routing study of \cref{ch:content}, which asks
which pages need a vision model, profiles all 1{,}593 pages of 25 lecture decks
by three signals each and so serves that interest in part. RQ1 and RQ3 came
from building the system.

\Cref{ch:background} gives the background and \cref{ch:system} an overview of
the system. \Cref{ch:content,ch:loop,ch:trust} answer RQ1 to RQ3, and
\cref{ch:conclusion} discusses the answers and their limits.

\section{Aims and Contributions}
\label{sec:aims}

The thesis has three aims, one per question. The first is to turn lecture
PDFs into structured pages while the original page stays what the learner
sees first. The second is to show the owner how the material is read without
showing any individual learner. The third is to check the answer keys of
generated quiz questions before learners see them and to measure how reliable
these checks are.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A rule that decides without a model call which pages go to a vision
    model, and a study that compares it with the old rule on all 1{,}593
    pages of 25 decks (\cref{ch:content}).
  \item A blind comparison of eleven text extraction methods on 24 pages from
    five PDFs: the author cast 283 votes with the method names
    hidden, under a protocol committed before the first vote
    (\cref{ch:content}). The comparison also reports each method's time and
    memory per page, measured on an 8\,GB Mac
    (\code{docs/\allowbreak thesis/\allowbreak experiments/\allowbreak parser-benchmark/\allowbreak PROTOCOL.md}).
  \item An event log, and an engine that reports to the owner patterns about
    pages that name no one and nothing about a page with fewer than five
    readers. We tested it with scripted readers, simulated members whose
    behaviour we planted (\cref{ch:loop}).
  \item A comparison of three checks of quiz answer keys on one probe: 16
    fixed pairs of a correct and a wrong answer key, which the author wrote
    with AI assistance. A lexical score, which measures how much of a key's wording
    occurs on its page, would drop 14 of the 16 correct keys. A similarity
    score, which compares the meaning of the page with that of the question
    and its key, failed its pre-registered test and was not adopted. A second model that answers without the key, the \emph{answer check}, made neither error
    in the calls that returned a verdict. On this probe every question had
    three off-topic options besides the key under test, and no criterion for
    the second model was fixed in advance (\cref{ch:trust}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Delimitation}
\label{sec:scope}

This is an engineering thesis about a system that runs in production. It
makes no claim about what students learn, because we ran no study with
learners and real use stayed too small to evaluate. In July 2026 the platform
was announced by e-mail to the participants of the Database Systems course.
Five accounts with university addresses signed up. Together they wrote 12
events, and none of them records a page read
(\code{docs/\allowbreak thesis/\allowbreak evidence/\allowbreak july-signups.md}).
In November 2026 the production database held 216 accounts, 55 of them on a
real mail or university domain, our own accounts and some test accounts among
them. Two of the 55 had written an event in the previous seven days, one of
them ours
(\code{docs/\allowbreak thesis/\allowbreak evidence/\allowbreak accounts-2026-11-10.md}).

Learnstation has more features than this thesis evaluates, among them Explain
and Ask on a page, the ask bar for a whole Space, a study assistant called
Luna with its spaced practice cards, and rehearsals, short mixed tests from a
Space's Lessons. The thesis describes them only where a research question
needs them.

Two students built Learnstation, and each of them submits a thesis on the shared code base. The co-developer created the repository in
January 2026; 53 of the 1{,}528 commits behind the code this thesis describes
are theirs, the last from August 2026. Their work includes the first content filter in three layers
(commit \code{bfbe6c09}), the integration of the Groq provider
(\code{76aa4b42}), and the first anonymised analytics for lecturers
(\code{3abfaa54}, \code{52ead4b5}). They also wired external extractors into
the fourth parser pipeline (\code{8aa255e2}), wrote most of a dashboard for
administrators (\code{0ef792dc}), and made about a dozen security fixes in
July 2026. This thesis covers the parts that the author built on this foundation: the
page-by-page pipeline and its benchmark, the event engine and the owner's
view, the verification of generated questions, and the interface in which
students read. Where a chapter describes something the co-developer wrote, it
says so. \Cref{tab:contrib} lists for each part who built it and the commits
that created it.

The thesis describes the code at one revision of the repository,
\code{\thesisCommit} of November 2026, which we froze for the thesis and call
\emph{the pin}. \emph{In the code} means reachable at the pin, and
\emph{deployed} means running in production. Since November 2026 production
runs \code{2e146f51}, the pin plus three later merges that change the code. Where one of these
merges matters, we name it and mark it as later. We checked the statements
about the system against the code at the pin, or at a later revision where a
section names one, and against production where the two can differ.
\Cref{app:detail} says where the evidence lies.

Security enters the thesis only where a research question needs it: the
privacy of members in the owner's view (\cref{ch:loop}) and instructions
hidden in uploaded pages (\cref{ch:trust}). We did not audit the security of
the system as a whole and ran no attack; attacks on the PDF library, denial of
service beyond the upload limits, and the hosting infrastructure lie outside
this thesis.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method-note}

We follow design science: we build an artefact for a stated problem and
evaluate it against that problem~\cite{hevner2004design}. Each of
\cref{ch:content,ch:loop,ch:trust} first describes what we built and then
evaluates it. We evaluate offline, on lecture material and scripted input
instead of learners, because almost no learner data arrived
(\cref{sec:scope}).

\Cref{tab:evaluations} lists the four evaluations. A comparison with an
expectation counts as a test only where we committed the expectation before
the run; otherwise we report it as a description. Each
chapter names the threats to the validity of its
evaluation~\cite{wohlin2012experimentation}.
